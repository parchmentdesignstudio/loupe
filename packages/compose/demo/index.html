Journal, no. 14

The press that prints the tide tables

For ninety years a two-room workshop above the harbour has set, proofed and printed the booklet every boat on this coast keeps in its wheelhouse.

Words by Imogen Hale, photographs by Tom Arkwright

The first thing you notice is the smell, which is ink and paraffin and something older underneath, and the second is that nobody in the room is in a hurry. The tables for next year were calculated in March, and the type has been standing in its formes since June, waiting on the paper.

Each page holds a month of high and low water for the six harbours between the two headlands. A mistake of a single digit could put a keel on the sand, so every figure is read aloud twice, once by the compositor and once by somebody who has never seen the page before.

“We have never once been late, and we have never once been early either.”

A forme for March, locked up on the stone and ready for its first proof

Counting the copies

The run has hardly changed since the war. What has changed is who buys it: fewer working boats each year, and more people who simply like to know when the causeway will be dry.

4,200 copies printed each November

312 pages of figures, all set by hand

1934 the year of the first edition

By the first week of December the bundles are on the quay, tied in twenties, and the workshop goes quiet until the spring calculations arrive.
